# Influences on The Woven Map

**By Dan Cross**  
*Creator of The Woven Map and Raven Calder*

---

## The Questions That Shaped the System

The Woven Map did not come from one school of thought. It grew out of a set of questions that other thinkers had already been asking in different ways.

Can a symbolic pattern correspond with human experience without physically causing it? Carl Jung wrestled with that question through synchronicity.

Can stories and symbols help people understand difficult periods without requiring those stories to be literally true? Joseph Campbell explored that territory through myth.

How easily do our models of reality become confused with reality itself? Alfred Korzybski and Robert Anton Wilson approached that problem through language and perception.

How do we take unusual experiences seriously without automatically treating them as either pathology or spiritual revelation? Ken Wilber offered useful distinctions there.

And perhaps most importantly for The Woven Map: what would make a symbolic claim capable of actually being wrong? Karl Popper's work on falsifiability provided an important challenge.

Astrologer Dane Rudhyar belongs in this lineage as well. His work helped move astrology away from simple fortune-telling and toward a symbolic description of processes unfolding through time.

The Woven Map borrows questions and useful tools from these thinkers. It does not adopt their philosophies wholesale, and their appearance here should not be taken as an endorsement of this system.

The common thread is simpler: **How can we use symbolic information without confusing it with fact, fate, or proof?**

That question shaped much of what followed.

## Carl Jung: Meaning Without a Known Cause

Carl Jung provides one of the clearest historical starting points.

Jung was interested in recurring symbolic patterns in dreams, myths, relationships, religion, and psychological life. More important for The Woven Map, however, was his idea of **synchronicity**: a meaningful correspondence between events that could not be explained through ordinary cause and effect.

That creates an interesting possibility for astrology.

The planets do not have to be imagined as remotely controlling someone's personality or making events happen. A narrower question can be asked instead:

**Does the astronomical pattern correspond with anything recognizable in human experience?**

The Woven Map does not claim to know why such a correspondence would exist.

Jung and physicist Wolfgang Pauli explored possibilities involving a deeper relationship between mind and matter. Other thinkers have proposed their own explanations since then. The Woven Map deliberately leaves that question unanswered.

If a correspondence appears, that is what can be examined.

If it does not appear, that matters too.

An unexplained result does not give us permission to invent an explanation.

### Allowing the Map to Miss

This is where The Woven Map goes in a different direction from purely interpretive approaches to synchronicity.

A symbolic system becomes very difficult to evaluate if every possible outcome can somehow be explained afterward.

Suppose the map describes a period of unusual pressure, but the person experiencing it reports an ordinary, easy day. We could always rescue the interpretation by saying the pressure was unconscious, internal, successfully integrated, or waiting to manifest.

The Woven Map does not allow that indefinitely.

Sometimes the map simply misses.

That possibility is essential because without it there is no meaningful way to distinguish an accurate correspondence from a clever explanation created after the fact.

### Behavior Rather Than Identity

Jung's work also influenced how The Woven Map handles difficult psychological material.

The system tries not to turn temporary behavior into permanent identity.

There is a substantial difference between saying:

**“You are controlling.”**

and:

**“When circumstances become uncertain, you may try to regain stability by controlling more of the situation.”**

The second statement describes something that can be recognized, rejected, or examined in context.

That distinction runs throughout The Woven Map. It is less interested in declaring what kind of person someone *is* than in asking what they tend to do under particular kinds of pressure.

### Houses as a Mandala, Not a Computational Dispute

Jung used natal charts in analytical work, but he did not treat house-system mathematics as a matter of dogma.

He left no required quadrant-versus-equal rule. In practice he defaulted to the systems available to him: **Campanus**, which his daughter Gret Baumann-Jung documented in the family collection and in his personal horoscopes, and **Placidus**, the Central European printed-table standard, which supplied the charts for the statistical tabulations in *Synchronicity: An Acausal Connecting Principle* (1952).

Campanus divides local space (the prime vertical). That spatial, equal-division geometry fits a mandala-like view of the sky. Placidus was simply what the manuals of the period used. Neither fact authorizes The Woven Map to claim that Jung invented, or would have endorsed, its twelve ordinary house labels.

What he did privilege is more useful here. He cared about the **four angles**—Ascendant, Descendant, Midheaven, and Imum Coeli—as the psychological bedrock of the chart: the horizon as the conscious self meeting the world, the meridian as ancestral or private roots versus visible public individuation. He used planets as archetypal markers and aspects as dynamic tension. A hard aspect carried more clinical weight than the exact degree boundary between, say, the 2nd and 3rd houses.

The Woven Map takes that as an interpretive priority, not a change to sealed house math. The twelve houses remain a mandala of experience—Identity, Money, Communication, Home, Play, and the rest are handles for where pressure can land. They are not an invitation to argue cusps in front of the reader.

A separate, later tradition — psychological astrology after Jung, especially Howard Sasportas and Liz Greene — is what “Jungian house meanings” refers to in the house table. That tradition interiorized the houses: from external areas of fate toward domains through which the psyche experiences life. It is not a claim that Jung himself wrote or endorsed the twelve frontstage handles.

## Joseph Campbell: Why Symbolic Language Still Matters

Joseph Campbell spent much of his career studying the stories people use to make sense of change, suffering, conflict, departure, loss, and renewal.

That matters because mathematical information alone does not necessarily help someone understand an experience.

An astrological calculation can tell us that two points form a particular angle.

Nobody experiences an angle.

People experience hesitation, conflict, attraction, exhaustion, opportunity, restraint, uncertainty, or relief.

Symbolic language can help translate between those two levels.

That is one of Raven Calder's primary jobs.

The important distinction is that **metaphor helps explain the information; it does not prove the information.**

Campbell's familiar patterns of departure, ordeal, transformation, and return can sometimes provide useful ways of thinking about a difficult period. But The Woven Map does not assume that every hardship is an initiation or that suffering necessarily happens for a reason.

Sometimes hardship is simply hardship.

A useful story can help someone navigate it without turning the story into a law of the universe.

## Robert Anton Wilson: Holding Models Loosely

Robert Anton Wilson repeatedly explored the ways expectations, language, culture, biology, and belief shape what people perceive.

He often described these frameworks as **reality tunnels**.

The idea is useful to The Woven Map because astrology itself remains a model.

So does The Woven Map.

A model can prove remarkably useful without becoming the same thing as reality.

That distinction places an important limit on what the system can claim.

An interpretation can feel remarkably specific without proving that astrology governs human life.

A repeated pattern can deserve further investigation without establishing its cause.

A metaphor can help someone understand an experience without becoming literal anatomy.

**Usefulness is not the same as proof.**

Wilson's interest in E-Prime also influenced Raven's language, although The Woven Map does not attempt to eliminate every form of the verb *to be*.

The more important lesson concerns identity.

Instead of declaring what someone *is*, Raven tries to describe what appears to be happening, under what conditions, and how the person might recognize it.

The distinction is small grammatically and enormous psychologically.

## Alfred Korzybski: The Map Is Not the Territory

Alfred Korzybski gave us one of the simplest statements behind the entire project:

**The map is not the territory.**

Every model leaves things out.

A birth chart leaves out almost everything about a person's life. An interpretation leaves out even more.

Problems begin when the model forgets its limitations.

The Woven Map therefore gives lived experience an important role. The chart can suggest something worth looking for, but the person experiencing the situation can say:

**No. That isn't what happened.**

The system should be able to accept that answer.

At the same time, someone's testimony cannot be allowed to change the original astronomical calculation until a match appears.

The map does not get to overrule reality.

Reality does not get to secretly redraw the map.

Keeping those two things separate became one of the system's most important design principles.

## Karl Popper: A System That Can Be Wrong

Karl Popper was not writing about astrology when he developed his ideas about falsifiability. His question nevertheless poses a serious challenge to any symbolic system.

**What result would show that the claim was wrong?**

Astrology has a particular problem here because symbolic language can stretch.

A difficult aspect might be interpreted as conflict. If there is no conflict, it can become internal conflict. If the person reports no internal conflict, perhaps the tension has been successfully integrated.

Continue long enough and nothing can ever count against the interpretation.

The Woven Map was deliberately designed to resist that tendency.

Its strongest methodological commitment is not that its interpretations are correct.

It is that **they must be allowed to be incorrect.**

### Who Speaks First?

This led to one of the more difficult questions in developing the system:

**Who should speak first—the person or the map?**

There is a problem either way.

If Raven gives an interpretation first, that interpretation may influence what someone notices or how they describe their experience afterward.

But if someone tells Raven everything that happened first, Raven already knows the answer. An interpretation can then sound remarkably precise simply because it has been shaped around information the person supplied.

The solution was to separate **calculation from conversation**.

The astronomical geometry is calculated before Raven interprets the person's testimony. That calculation does not change because of what the person subsequently says.

From there, the conversational order can vary.

During an exploratory session, it can make sense for someone to describe what is happening before seeing Raven's interpretation. This helps reduce suggestion.

During a more deliberate test, Raven can interpret the previously calculated map without first knowing what happened. The interpretation can then be compared with the person's independent account.

These approaches guard against different problems.

One limits suggestion.

The other limits retrospective fitting.

The underlying rule remains the same:

**The calculation should not move merely because the story changed.**

## Ken Wilber: Taking Unusual Experience Seriously Without Automatically Believing It

Ken Wilber contributes a useful warning about unusual experiences.

Something can fall outside ordinary expectations for very different reasons. An experience might involve confusion, impaired judgment, or psychological distress. Or it might simply be difficult to describe using ordinary categories.

Two mistakes become possible.

We can dismiss every unusual experience as irrational.

Or we can treat every unusual experience as evidence of spiritual revelation.

The Woven Map tries to do neither.

A powerful experience proves that someone had a powerful experience. It does not, by itself, establish what caused it.

A remarkable coincidence can remain remarkable without automatically becoming a message from the universe.

Likewise, symbolic interpretation should never erase ordinary practical evidence.

If someone describes an experience as spiritually transformative while also going without sleep, becoming unable to function, or placing themselves in danger, the symbolic story does not outrank those facts.

Different ways of understanding an experience can coexist, but they should not impersonate one another.

## Dane Rudhyar: Astrology as Process Rather Than Fate

Dane Rudhyar provides one of the most direct astrological influences.

His work helped move twentieth-century astrology away from simple prediction and toward understanding a chart as a symbolic description of an unfolding life.

The Woven Map shares that general direction.

It treats the chart as a connected structure rather than a collection of isolated personality traits. Planets acquire meaning through their positions and relationships. The birth chart provides one reference point; the changing sky provides another.

But The Woven Map places tighter limits on interpretation.

A chart does not automatically tell us that a marriage will end, someone will become wealthy, an illness will occur, or a career opportunity will arrive.

It describes symbolic conditions.

What actually happens under those conditions depends on far more than astrology.

That distinction is central to the system.

---

## James Hillman: People Can Contain Contradictions

James Hillman's archetypal psychology provides another useful corrective.

People do not always behave as though they possess one motive, one direction, and one perfectly unified self.

Someone can genuinely want intimacy and independence.

Security and change.

Recognition and privacy.

Continuity and reinvention.

The Woven Map does not assume that one side must represent the person's “real self.”

Sometimes the contradiction itself is the important information.

Hillman's work also influenced earlier Woven Map ideas about an underlying pattern developing across a life. The current system treats that possibility cautiously.

A birth chart can provide a stable reference for comparison.

It is not proof of a predetermined destiny or hidden spiritual blueprint.

The image remains useful.

The metaphysical conclusion remains optional.

---

## Wolfgang Pauli: Being Curious Without Inventing an Answer

Wolfgang Pauli enters this history largely through his conversations and collaboration with Jung.

What matters here is not an appeal to Pauli's authority as a physicist.

It is the willingness to investigate an unusual correspondence without pretending that curiosity itself establishes the mechanism.

That distinction remains important because astrology is frequently defended with references to quantum mechanics, entanglement, consciousness fields, or other ideas borrowed from physics.

The Woven Map does not make those claims.

Quantum physics does not currently provide the system with an established explanation for astrological correspondence.

The appropriate position is much simpler:

**Do not dismiss an interesting observation merely because its explanation remains unknown. Do not invent an explanation merely because the observation interests you.**

---

## David Bohm and Rupert Sheldrake: Interesting Possibilities, Not Evidence

David Bohm and Rupert Sheldrake have influenced some of the speculative thinking surrounding The Woven Map.

Bohm explored the possibility that visible separation might not describe the deepest level of physical reality. Sheldrake has proposed controversial ideas about recurring patterns across biological systems.

Both can provoke interesting questions about pattern and relationship.

Neither provides scientific evidence that astrology works.

That distinction matters enough to state plainly.

The Woven Map does not need quantum consciousness, morphic fields, or any other speculative mechanism to perform its calculations or compare an interpretation with someone's experience.

Questions about an ultimate mechanism remain open.

---

## Shawn Carlson: Keeping the Skeptic in the Room

Shawn Carlson's well-known 1985 double-blind astrology study belongs in this history because skepticism helped shape The Woven Map too.

The study challenged the idea that astrologers could reliably match birth charts with personality profiles under controlled conditions. Its methods and interpretation have been debated since publication, but the larger challenge remains useful.

If astrology produces descriptions broad enough to fit almost anyone, apparent accuracy tells us very little.

The Woven Map does not answer that criticism by declaring that traditional astrology failed while The Woven Map succeeds.

Instead, it asks a narrower question:

**Can we make symbolic claims specific enough that they can actually miss?**

That is a much smaller claim.

It is also a more useful one to test.

---

## Albert Camus: Meaning Without Cosmic Reassurance

Albert Camus influenced the philosophical posture of The Woven Map more than its technical design.

The system does not assume that suffering happens for a reason.

A catastrophe does not necessarily arrive to teach someone a lesson.

Pain does not prove karmic debt, failed manifestation, spiritual weakness, or divine intention.

Sometimes terrible things happen in an indifferent world.

A map can still have value.

Its purpose does not have to be explaining why the landscape exists. Sometimes its purpose is simply helping someone understand where they are and decide where to go next.

Agency does not require cosmic reassurance.

# What The Woven Map Adds

These influences explain where some of the questions came from.

They do not explain the entire system.

Several principles emerged specifically through the development of The Woven Map.

## Calculation Before Interpretation

The astronomical geometry is calculated independently of the user's story.

This is the simplest and most important technical rule in the system.

A person's testimony may affect what Raven discusses with them.

It does not alter where the planets were or what geometric relationships were present.

## Different Ways of Testing the Same Map

Sometimes the person speaks first.

Sometimes Raven does.

The choice depends on what is being tested.

When reducing suggestion matters most, the person's account can be recorded before Raven reveals the interpretation.

When testing Raven's interpretation itself, Raven can speak before learning what happened.

The calculation underneath both approaches remains unchanged.

## A Real Miss

The system needs more than *yes* and *sort of*.

It also needs *no*.

The Woven Map therefore preserves a category for interpretations that fall outside what the person actually experienced.

A miss should not automatically trigger another interpretation designed to rescue the first one.

## Birth Chart and Changing Conditions

The system distinguishes between the chart calculated for someone's birth and the changing astronomical conditions at another point in time.

A useful everyday analogy is a landscape and its weather.

The landscape does not dictate what will happen there.

The weather does not determine how every person will respond.

But the interaction between stable conditions and changing conditions can still be worth examining.

## Raven Calder

Raven exists to make the calculated information understandable.

She does not calculate a more convenient chart when the first interpretation fails.

She does not decide what someone must have experienced.

Her job is translation.

That translation can sometimes use metaphor, humor, or symbolic language. At other times, plain language works better.

Either way, the interpretation remains accountable to the calculation underneath it and to the person evaluating whether it actually fits.

# The Simplest Synthesis

If there is a short intellectual description of The Woven Map, **“Jung meets Popper”** still works surprisingly well.

Jung leaves open the possibility that meaningful correspondence may exist without ordinary causation.

Popper asks what would allow us to discover that our interpretation was wrong.

Campbell helps explain why symbolic language can remain useful even when it is not literal.

Wilson and Korzybski remind us not to mistake our models for reality.

Wilber cautions against treating every unusual experience as either pathology or revelation.

Rudhyar provides an important precedent for astrology understood as process rather than fate.

Hillman leaves room for contradiction within a person.

Pauli represents disciplined curiosity about unexplained correspondence.

Carlson keeps skepticism inside the conversation.

Camus removes the requirement that the universe must care about us for navigation to matter.

Bohm and Sheldrake remain interesting possibilities at the edge of the discussion rather than foundations underneath it.

The Woven Map takes these questions and applies a simple discipline:

**Calculate the map independently.**

**Say clearly what it appears to suggest.**

**Compare that with what actually happened.**

**Allow the answer to be no.**

**Do not invent a mechanism we cannot demonstrate.**

**And never confuse the map with the person holding it.**

The Woven Map is not an attempt to prove that astrology secretly controls human life.

It asks a smaller and, I think, more interesting question:

**Can an old symbolic system become more useful when we become stricter about what it is allowed to claim?**

That is the experiment.
